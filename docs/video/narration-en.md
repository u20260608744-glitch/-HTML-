# Woodlands — concise English presentation

## One brief, three garden futures

Woodlands explores three garden communities for sixty homes in Lusaka, Zambia. The same brief sets a five-metre setback and a developed area below ten thousand square metres. We rotate the actual source model to compare architectural relationships, rather than treating rendered imagery as a finished design.

## Reading the place

Four site readings connect location, access, amenities and surrounding heights. The parcel sits within a connected street network, near schools, services and open spaces. The height graphic describes mainly one-to-two-storey housing, with smaller three-to-four-storey patches. Its tinted areas and suggested height ranges are study interpretations, not a measured survey.

## City access, garden life

The community ambition is clear: connected to the city, and at home in a garden. Three responses shape that ambition: a recognizable, quieter arrival; shared places for everyday encounters; and homes benefiting from planting, outlook and privacy.

## Three architectural arrangements

Point Courtyard distributes four blocks around garden spaces, opening views while requiring careful spacing and repeated cores. Linear Terrace gives the site a clear edge and connected outdoor setting, with apartment depth and corridor daylight to resolve. Courtyard Block focuses shared life within a connected form; inner corners, overlooking and terrace boundaries need study.

## Plans and development figures

All three plans use the reported site area of 5,838.7 square metres. Their overall totals are 9,748, 9,622 and 9,658 square metres, respectively. Derived floor-area ratios are approximately 1.39, 1.37 and 1.38; footprint densities are 31.3, 27 and 27 percent. These are concept estimates. Linear Terrace offers the most headroom below the brief limit. Each includes 1,620 square metres of reported basement parking: roughly thirty-five to forty-five spaces by area allowance, pending a real layout.

## Understanding the vertical relationship

The sections connect upper homes, lower amenities and shared outdoor life. Program colours, marker and pencil retain the same source cuts, while distant elements remain lighter. Missing interior partitions or structural details are not invented.

## Five roles, read together

Across all three options, five linked highlights identify residential space, amenities, landscape, water, and arrival. They reveal how homes meet gardens, pools and common routes. Every scheme links its five categories differently: open gaps in OP1, a continuous garden edge in OP2, and shared platforms in OP3. The coloured model and explanations remain visible together; rotation supports spatial reading, and optional studio shadows clarify depth.

## Learning from built homes

Three references sharpen the questions. Berlin’s OASIS suggests garden spaces between separate homes. TOMAZ in Porto Alegre connects residential edges with planting and shared leisure. Guadalajara’s La Toscana organizes common ground and terraces. These are lessons to adapt locally, not designs or performance results to copy.

## Comparing evidence and priorities

Comparison combines reported areas with spatial judgments. Linear Terrace has the greatest area allowance; Point Courtyard has the largest residential area. Priority sliders explore garden life, context, privacy, simplicity and headroom. Changing those priorities changes the ranking: it remains a concept assessment, not objective proof.

## The next design decision

The next choice is which relationships to develop and test. Dwelling plans, access, daylight, privacy, landscape, structure and parking will determine their quality. The interactive book keeps the three alternatives, their evidence and their unresolved questions available for that work.
